# 01 Why This Guide Exists

I had a GitHub account for years.

The account was there. So were the ideas. I kept stopping at the same point: I could imagine a project, but I could not build the part that made it real because I did not know how to code.

Then I started using Codex.

I am still the person who writes stories and makes things for people to read. Codex helps move the work forward: reading files, organizing material, creating repositories, changing pages, running checks, and leaving the result behind. In August 2026, I was maintaining more than 100 GitHub repositories this way, covering essays, people, music, and science.

This guide is for people like me: people with ideas, taste, and creative ability who hesitate when a project turns into folders, commands, and repositories.

## The first thing I learned

I used to think of Codex as a tool that wrote a few lines of code. That was too small a view.

I now treat it as a work partner that can take an assignment. I explain what I want, and it can read the situation before moving the work forward. I decide what matters, what is true, and whether the final result is ready. Codex handles the tedious middle: reading, editing, organizing, checking, and preparing the handoff.

Here are a few things I have actually done:

- My GitHub profile had reached 102 repositories, while the badges in my introduction still said 84. Codex checked the real numbers, changed the three lines that needed changing, and helped me verify the live page.
- I had 681 AI-related bookmarks spread across 32 pages. Codex helped identify the pagination, process the pages, and narrow the collection to 22 items about using Codex.
- I was preparing a Codex for OSS application. Codex helped inspect the form fields and compress the writing to under 500 characters. I kept adding my real story and checking every fact.
- I turned essays into public repositories and set up GitHub Actions to update one project every day. The work keeps growing after publication.

The common thread is simple. I did not wait until I had learned programming. I stated the idea, let Codex make a first pass, kept correcting it, checked important facts myself, and saved the useful method for next time.

## What remains your job

Using Codex well does not require a long list of technical terms.

You need to do four things:

1. State the result you actually want.
2. Put the material it needs within reach.
3. Say which files it may change and which must stay untouched.
4. Open the result and check that guesses have not been presented as facts.

Codex can draft, organize, edit files, and run checks. You decide whether something is accurate, safe to publish, or ready to share.

## How to read this guide

The chapters follow real situations:

- turn a vague sentence into a clear assignment;
- help Codex understand your folder and working habits;
- save a useful operation as a rule or a Skill;
- let a project update itself, and learn how to stop it when something goes wrong.

Each chapter answers three questions: what needed solving, what was said to Codex, and where to look when the work is done. You can try each chapter in your own folder without learning everything first.

## Start with a small exercise

Open a blank file and write three sentences:

```text
I want Codex to help me:
The material I already have is:
I will know it worked when:
```

Choose something real: organize meeting notes, publish an essay to GitHub, or give a group of files a consistent format.

Those three sentences are enough to begin. The next chapter turns them into an assignment Codex can follow.
